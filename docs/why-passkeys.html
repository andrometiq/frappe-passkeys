Authentication, from first principles

Why passkeys are safer — and when they aren't

A ground-up account of what actually makes a login strong: the three factors, why anything you can type can be stolen, the two cryptographic locks behind phishing resistance, and the honest limits. Written for a developer who knows HTTP and crypto primitives but never sat down with auth theory.

00 · The puzzle

Your bank feels like a checkpoint. Google feels like nothing. One of them is wrong about what security is.

Signing into your bank can mean an OTP, then a card PIN, sometimes a security question — and it still feels fragile. Signing into Google is a single glance, and it feels bulletproof. Intuition says more hoops mean more safety. Intuition is backwards.

This page is about what actually makes authentication strong. The punchline, up front: the strong thing usually makes your life easier, not harder. Friction and security are not the same lever, and the industry spent twenty years confusing them.

The bank — visible friction

SMS OTP card PIN security Q captcha

Three prompts, and the strongest of them (SMS) is the one an attacker can catch.

Google — invisible strength

One glance. Face or fingerprint, done — and it is arguably the safer of the two.

Takeaway Friction is not security. The rest of this page is about what is.

01 · The primitives

Every login is built from three factors

Authentication proves you are who you claim by testing one or more of exactly three things:

  • Knowledge — something you know. A password, PIN, passphrase, or security-question answer. Its weakness is intrinsic: it can be told, phished, guessed, reused across sites, or leaked in a breach.
  • Possession — something you have. A phone, a hardware key, a SIM, an authenticator-app instance, or the private key of a passkey. Its strength varies wildly with how possession is proven — a code you retype is weak possession; a key that signs and never leaves the device is strong possession.
  • Inherence — something you are. A fingerprint, face, iris, or voice. Its weakness: it is not secret (your face is public), not revocable (you can't reset a fingerprint), and only as good as the sensor's liveness detection.

Marketing counts factors — "two-factor!", "multi-factor!". Security cares about their quality. Two weak factors can be worse than one strong one.

Takeaway Every login is some mix of these three. The quality of each factor matters more than the count.

Orientation · The landscape

Where every common method actually sits

Here is the whole field on one ladder, weakest at the bottom. There is a hard line across the middle — the phishing-resistant line. Below it, you hand the site something an attacker can catch and reuse. Above it, your device signs a one-time challenge and hands over nothing reusable at all. Everything else on this page is really an explanation of that line.

02 · The bottom rung

Why passwords lose

A password fails on three independent counts, any one of which is fatal:

  • It is typeable, therefore stealable. The whole security of a password rests on it going only to the right site. But you can type it into any page, and a lookalike page will happily take it. Your eyes decide where it goes, and your eyes lose to a convincing copy.
  • It is reusable. The same secret works every time, so a copy captured once works forever — until you rotate it, which most people never do.
  • The server keeps a copy worth stealing. Even hashed, a leaked password database can be cracked offline at leisure (more in section 06).

The classic phish is dumbed-down on purpose — a domain that reads almost right:

https://example.com the real site
https://examp1e.com (that's a numeral "1") looks identical, isn't

Whatever you type into the second bar goes to whoever served that page. A password can't tell the difference; a passkey (section 05) can, and won't even appear.

Takeaway Anything you can type into a page, an attacker can capture from a fake one.

03 · The trap that looks like 2FA

The 2FA that isn't: OTP, TOTP, and push

Adding a second factor feels like a fix, and for offline password lists it helps. But the popular second factors share the password's fatal flaw — they still route a secret through the user, who can be fooled about the destination.

  • SMS / email OTP. A code you retype. A real-time proxy phishing kit puts a page between you and the real site, forwards your login, and relays the OTP the instant you enter it. SIM-swap grabs the code outright.
  • TOTP authenticator apps. Better than SMS (no SIM-swap, no carrier), but it is still a code you retype into a page. The proxy relays it inside the 30-second window. The math is stronger; the human bottleneck is identical.
  • Push approval. No code to type, so it feels safest — but the "Approve?" tap carries no information about which site asked. Trigger enough prompts and a tired user taps yes on the attacker's login. That's MFA-fatigue / push-bombing.

The common defect: none of these knows which origin it is talking to. The user is the origin check, and the user can be phished.

Takeaway A code you retype — or a blind "Approve?" tap — has no idea which site it's talking to.

04 · The mechanism

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is a public/private key pair created per site. The two halves live in very different places, and that split is the whole point:

  • Possession — the private key. It sits in the device's secure hardware (Secure Enclave, TPM, StrongBox) or a provider's end-to-end-encrypted keychain. Proving you can sign with it proves possession. It never transmits.
  • User verification — the local unlock. Before signing, the authenticator makes you unlock locally with a biometric (inherence) or the device PIN (knowledge). Crucially, the biometric or PIN never leaves the device and is never sent to the site. It only releases the local key; the site just sees a "user-verified" flag inside the signed data.

So one Face-ID glance delivers a phishing-resistant possession proof plus evidence a human was present — two factors, one gesture. And the server only ever holds the public key, which is safe to leak.

Takeaway The secret never leaves your device; the server only ever holds a public key that's useless to steal.

05 · The centerpiece

Phishing resistance, mechanically: two independent locks

"Phishing-resistant" is not a marketing adjective here; it is two separate cryptographic mechanisms, each of which would defeat the phish on its own.

Lock 1 — Origin binding: the fake site can't even ask

Every passkey is scoped to a Relying Party ID — the real domain, say example.com. The browser and OS enforce that a passkey registered for example.com is only ever offered when the page's origin genuinely matches. On evil-example.com, the browser will not surface the example.com passkey at all. The ceremony can't even start for the attacker's domain. There is nothing for the user to fat-finger.

Lock 2 — Challenge signing: a captured response is worthless

The server sends a fresh random challenge. The browser assembles clientDataJSON = { type, challenge, origin }, and the authenticator signs authenticatorData ‖ SHA-256(clientDataJSON) — so both the origin and the challenge are sealed inside the signature. If an attacker somehow relayed an assertion, it would carry the attacker's origin, and the real server rejects unknown origins. The challenge is single-use, so nothing can be replayed later. And there is no reusable secret to capture in the first place: the user types nothing, and the private key never transmits.

# what the authenticator actually signs
signature = sign(authenticatorData ‖ SHA-256(clientDataJSON))
clientDataJSON = { type, challenge: <fresh random>, origin: "https://example.com" }

06 · What a server leak yields

Breaches, stuffing, and replay

The phishing story is about the live login. This one is about the day the server's database leaks — and here too the passkey model changes what an attacker walks away with.

  • Breach. A passkey server stores only the public key. You cannot forge signatures from a public key, so the dump is inert. Compare a password database: even hashed, it can be cracked offline at the attacker's leisure.
  • Credential stuffing. There is no shared secret to reuse, and each passkey is unique per site, so a breach at site A hands over nothing to try at site B. Password reuse is the entire reason stuffing works; passkeys remove the reuse.
  • Replay. The per-attempt random challenge means a captured assertion can't be replayed later, and WebAuthn carries an optional signature counter to flag cloned authenticators.

Takeaway Nothing worth stealing is stored, nothing is reused across sites, nothing can be replayed.

07 · Cross-device

Using your phone's passkey on a borrowed screen (the QR flow)

One name, three words, so you're not confused later: the mechanism was caBLE ("cloud-assisted BLE"); folded into the CTAP 2.2 spec it was renamed hybrid (the WebAuthn transport string is literally hybrid); marketing calls it cross-device authentication. Same thing.

The job: sign in on a machine that doesn't hold your passkey — a borrowed laptop, a public desktop, a different ecosystem than your phone. The desktop shows a QR code; your phone (which holds the key) completes the ceremony; the desktop gets logged in and the private key never leaves the phone. Condensed, the steps are:

  1. The desktop has no local passkey, so it renders a QR code encoding a short-lived, single-use handshake (a FIDO:/ URI: an ephemeral P-256 public key plus a 16-byte shared secret).
  2. Your phone scans it — the native camera recognizes the FIDO: URI.
  3. The phone broadcasts one BLE advertisement, phone → desktop — the only Bluetooth step — encrypted with the shared secret, carrying a nonce and which tunnel server to meet at.
  4. The desktop hears and decrypts that advert (this proves proximity), then dials the named tunnel server over the internet.
  5. An end-to-end-encrypted tunnel forms through the relay; the CTAP2 ceremony runs over it; your phone prompts for Face ID / fingerprint / PIN, signs, and returns the assertion. The desktop hands it to the site. You're in.

Why the Bluetooth step is the anti-phishing part

Bluetooth here is a proximity proof, not a data channel — no challenge, key, or credential rides over BLE; it all goes through the encrypted internet tunnel. As Adam Langley put it: "if we can send one Bluetooth message from the phone and make the computer prove that it has received it, then all other communication can be routed over the Internet." That single short-range advert is the trick. A phishing site can relay a QR code to a victim anywhere on earth and get them to scan it — but the passkey only completes if the screen showing the QR is physically next to the phone, because the desktop must prove it heard the advert. A phisher's server on another continent can't hear a Bluetooth advert, so the ceremony dies.

Takeaway Bluetooth just proves the two devices are in the same room — which kills remote phishing; the real work rides an encrypted internet tunnel and your key never moves.

08 · The honest part

What passkeys do not fix

A page that only sells you the wins is a brochure. Passkeys are excellent at one specific thing — making the login itself phishing-proof — and they leave real gaps elsewhere. Know them, or you'll build a false sense of safety.

Someone already unlocked your device

Anyone who can unlock your phone — knows the PIN, or defeats Face ID — can use every passkey on it. The passkey trusts the local unlock; it does not re-check the human beyond that.

A weak recovery back door

Most sites keep flimsy fallbacks alive — email link, SMS OTP, an old password. An attacker skips the passkey and walks the easiest path. Passkeys stop phishing; your fallbacks quietly undo it.

Your cloud account got taken over

Synced passkeys live in your Apple / Google / Microsoft account. Providers end-to-end-encrypt the sync (they can't read your keys), but if that account is compromised, the keys can be misused. Security now rests on that account's own MFA.

Two more, quieter: losing your recovery key (iCloud Keychain recovery can then take days) and plain sync failures that leave a passkey unavailable on a device — an availability headache, not a breach, but real UX pain.

Takeaway Passkeys make the front door pick-proof. They do nothing about someone already inside your device, and nothing about a flimsy back door (recovery) you left unlocked.

09 · The payoff

So why does my bank pile on hoops and Google doesn't?

Now the opening puzzle has an answer. The bank's visible friction and Google's invisible ease come from opposite philosophies, and the quieter one is arguably the safer.

Why banks pile on visible friction

  • Regulation mandates it. The EU's PSD2 Strong Customer Authentication requires at least two of the three factors for online payments; India's RBI mandates 2FA for all domestic digital payment transactions from 1 April 2026. That's a legal floor, not a UX choice.
  • Dynamic linking. PSD2 further requires the authentication be bound to the specific amount and payee, so each payment tends to trigger its own visible challenge by design.
  • Liability shifting. Passing SCA / 3-D Secure shifts fraud liability toward the issuer or customer. Some of that friction is a legal artifact — "we made you prove it" — not pure security.
  • Legacy cores and auditability. Banks run decades-old systems and answer to auditors. A visible, logged OTP is easy to point at in an audit; an invisible risk engine is hard to certify to a regulator.

The irony: the factor they mandate is often SMS-OTP — phishable and SIM-swappable. RBI itself is now steering away from SMS-OTP toward "deterministic, dynamic, and phishing-resistant" methods. The regulators agree the visible friction they created isn't the strong part.

Why Google feels frictionless yet is arguably safer

Google doesn't skip authentication; it moves most of it into the background. A risk engine scores every sign-in from many signals — device fingerprint, IP and geolocation, historical behavior, session reputation, bot signals — and only interrupts you when the score looks off. Low-risk logins are silent. And your already-authenticated, remembered device is itself quietly acting as a possession factor, so a re-challenge is rarely needed. The strong factor is there; it just isn't making you type anything.

The honest answer to "isn't it the same trade-off?"

No — security and usability are not the same lever, and friction is a terrible proxy for security. The strong part of a login is the nature of the factor, not how much the user suffers. A phishing-resistant possession factor plus a smart risk engine beats a pile of visible-but-phishable hoops. The evidence is blunt: Google put physical security keys in front of 85,000+ employees in 2018 and has had zero successful phishing since — and it was also faster and cheaper (~50% quicker than SMS-OTP, ~4× faster than TOTP). The same posture is why Cloudflare shrugged off the 2022 campaign that breached Twilio (FIDO2 keys, all other MFA disabled), and why Snap reported zero account takeovers for over two years.

Better auth removes friction and adds security at once — you were never supposed to trade them. Fair caveat: banks carry regulatory and liability constraints Google's consumer login doesn't, and invisible risk-scoring has its own costs (false positives, opacity, harder audits). But the direction of travel — RBI and PSD2 both moving toward phishing-resistant and risk-based auth — vindicates the passkey thesis, not the pile of hoops.

When a second factor still matters Passkeys don't retire the second factor everywhere. Keep one where a regulator requires it (payments under PSD2 / RBI), for high-value or sensitive step-up actions (changing recovery settings, moving money, admin operations), and for as long as weaker recovery fallbacks remain enabled — the fallback is only as strong as the day you finally disable it.

Takeaway Better auth gives you both less friction and more security — you were never meant to trade them.

10 · The close

What this means for a Frappe app

Putting passkeys into a Frappe site is choosing the Google model over the bank model: authentication that is phishing-resistant by construction and easier for users than the password it replaces. The frappe-passkeys app brings origin-bound, challenge-signed WebAuthn login to Frappe — the two locks from section 05, applied to your own login screen. The server stops storing a crackable secret and starts storing a public key that's safe to leak.

Keep the honest caveats from section 08 in view: harden or disable the weak recovery fallbacks so a passkey login isn't quietly undone by an email-link back door, and keep a second factor where regulation or a sensitive action demands it. Do that, and the login is both stronger and lighter — the rare change that costs the user nothing.
