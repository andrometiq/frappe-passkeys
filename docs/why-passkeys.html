A plain-language guide

Why passkeys are safer — and when they aren't

A passkey lets you sign in with your fingerprint, face, or screen lock — no password to type, remember, or leak. It's the rare security upgrade that also makes signing in easier. Here's what it fixes, in plain terms, and the few things it doesn't — so you can decide with your eyes open.

Nothing to phish There's no password or code to type, so a fake site has nothing to capture.
Nothing to breach The site stores only a public key — useless to steal, and unique to that one site.
Faster, too One glance or touch replaces typing a password — and it's quicker than an OTP.

What it is

A passkey replaces your password with a key on your device

To sign in, you unlock it the way you already unlock your phone — a fingerprint, your face, or a PIN. That's the whole experience. Underneath, a passkey is a pair of keys, and the split is the point:

  • The private key lives in your device's secure hardware and never leaves it. Signing in proves you hold it, without ever sending it.
  • The fingerprint or PIN unlocks that key locally. It is never sent to the site either — the site only learns that “a real person unlocked this device.”
  • The site keeps only the matching public key, which is safe to leak.

So one touch delivers two things at once — proof you have the device, and proof a human is present — and neither secret ever reaches the site.

In short The secret never leaves your device; the server only ever holds a public key that's useless to steal.

Why it's safer

Two ways a password gets stolen — and a passkey closes both

1. Phishing: nothing to type means nothing to hand over. A passkey is tied to the real site it was made for. Your browser will only offer it on that exact site — on a lookalike phishing page, the passkey simply isn't offered, so the attacker can't even ask. A password or a one-time code is different: you can type it into any page, and a convincing fake will happily take it. This is the big one, because even “two-factor” codes (SMS, authenticator apps, “Approve?” taps) are still things a fooled user hands to the wrong site.

2. Breaches: nothing worth stealing is stored. The server keeps only public keys, which can't be used to sign in. There's no password hash to crack at leisure, and because each passkey is unique to one site, a leak at one place is worthless everywhere else — the reuse that makes “credential stuffing” work is gone.

Here's where the common methods actually sit. There's a hard line in the middle: below it you hand over something an attacker can catch and reuse; above it, your device signs a one-time challenge and hands over nothing.

In short A fake site can't even ask your passkey, and a server breach leaks only public keys — worthless to an attacker.

The intuition-buster

So why does my bank pile on hoops and Google doesn't?

Signing into your bank can mean an OTP, a card PIN, maybe a security question — and it still feels fragile. Signing into Google is a single glance, and it feels bulletproof. The lesson isn't “Google is careless.” It's that friction is not the same thing as security.

Why banks show visible hoops

Mostly it isn't a security choice: regulation requires it (the EU's PSD2 and India's RBI both mandate two factors for payments), it shifts fraud liability, and decades-old systems plus auditors favour a visible, logged code. The irony is that the factor they most often mandate — SMS OTP — is itself phishable and SIM-swappable, and regulators like RBI are now steering away from it toward phishing-resistant methods.

Why Google feels effortless yet is safer

Google didn't skip authentication — it moved the work into the background. A risk engine quietly scores each sign-in (device, location, behaviour) and only interrupts you when something looks off, on top of a device-bound key. The strong factor is there; it just isn't making you type anything.

The evidence is blunt: Google put security keys in front of 85,000+ employees in 2018 and has had zero successful phishing since — and it was faster and cheaper than the codes it replaced. Security and convenience were never the same lever. Better authentication gives you both.

In short Friction is a terrible proxy for security. Passkeys give your users less friction and more safety at the same time.

When they aren't enough

What passkeys don't fix

Passkeys are excellent at one specific thing — making the login itself phishing-proof. They leave real gaps elsewhere. If you're deciding whether to turn them on, these are the ones to plan around.

Someone already unlocked the device

Anyone who can unlock the phone — knows the PIN, or gets past Face ID — can use every passkey on it. The passkey trusts that local unlock.

A weak recovery back door

An email link, an SMS code, or an old password left switched on lets an attacker skip the passkey entirely. Passkeys stop phishing; a flimsy fallback quietly undoes it.

A hijacked cloud account

Synced passkeys live in an Apple / Google / Microsoft account (end-to-end encrypted). If that account itself is taken over, its own security is what protects the keys.

In short Passkeys make the front door pick-proof. Close the side doors too — disable weak recovery fallbacks, and keep a second factor where it counts.

For your Frappe site

Turning this on

Adding passkeys to a Frappe site is choosing the same model as Google: sign-in that's phishing-resistant by construction, and lighter for your users than the password it replaces. The server stops storing a crackable secret and starts storing a public key that's safe to leak.

Two things make it count. Turn on a passkey login mode, then harden the fallbacks around it — the Passkey Settings page flags any way a passkey can still be bypassed (password login, social sign-in, email links) so you can close them. And keep a second factor where a regulator or a sensitive action calls for one. Do that, and the login is both stronger and easier — the rare change that costs your users nothing.
